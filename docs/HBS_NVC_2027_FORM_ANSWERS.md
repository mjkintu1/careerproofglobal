## Business Model (200 word limit)

> CareerProof Global is free for students and job seekers, always. Charging young people would shut out the students who need guidance most, so revenue comes from organisations that want to reach, support or understand them.
>
> 1. Secondary schools: a counsellor dashboard and class reports, sold as a termly subscription priced per enrolled student. This is our first paid product.
> 2. Universities and colleges: verified programme listings and anonymised insight into which careers and courses students are exploring.
> 3. Employers and scholarship providers: reaching students and graduates who match their requirements, with internship and graduate-job postings.
> 4. Governments, NGOs and researchers: anonymised, aggregated data on subject choices and career interest, to plan education and skills programmes.
>
> Costs stay low: the core data comes from official open sources (the ILO, UNESCO, the EU's ESCO, national statistics offices and ministries) that we cite rather than buy, and the platform is a lightweight web app that works on basic smartphones. Each new country reuses the same structure: we add its exams, admission rules, pay scales and institutions as data, not new software, so entering a market is cheap and the school subscription can be profitable at modest volumes.

## Market Opportunity: Customers, Market size, Scalability (200 word limit)

> Customers: secondary schools (our paying customer) and the students they serve; then universities, employers and scholarship providers who want to reach those students.
>
> Market size: Africa had 104.7 million secondary-school students in 2024 (UNESCO Institute for Statistics), 78 million of them in sub-Saharan Africa. In the countries we target next: Kenya 6.7 million (2023), Ghana 3.3 million (2024), Rwanda 0.9 million (2024), and Nigeria 15.2 million (2023). At a school licence of US$2 per student per year, the addressable market is about US$209 million a year, before university, employer and data revenue.
>
> Need: every one of these students must choose subjects and courses, often with no careers adviser. Curriculum reforms in Uganda (competency-based lower secondary) and Kenya (new senior-school pathways from 2026) make those choices newer and harder for families.
>
> Scalability: the platform is built on international standards (the ILO's ISCO-08 occupations, UNESCO's ISCED levels), so every country already has a basic version: careers, institutions and official pay for 170 countries. Going deep in a new country means adding its exams, admission rules and pay scales as data. Uganda is our pilot; Kenya, Rwanda, Tanzania, Ghana and Nigeria follow.

## Go-to-Market Strategy (200 word limit)

> Phase 1, Uganda pilot (now to March 2027): we work with 3 to 5 secondary schools through their careers teachers and S4 to S6 classes, timed around subject choice and the university admission cycle. Teachers use the path planner and the Uganda points calculator in class; we collect feedback and measure whether students change or confirm their choices. **[FILL IN: schools already approached]**
>
> Phase 2, first paid schools: we convert pilot schools to a termly subscription for the counsellor dashboard and class reports, and use their results to sign neighbouring schools through head-teacher and careers-teacher networks.
>
> Data partnerships: we approach the National Council for Higher Education, the Public Universities Joint Admissions Board and university admissions offices in Uganda, and KUCCPS in Kenya, for admission rules and cut-offs. Partnerships make the data better and give us credibility with schools.
>
> Organic reach: thousands of career, occupation, subject and curriculum pages built on official data attract students through search. Shareable route maps spread through WhatsApp, and results-season campaigns reach students when they are deciding.
>
> Phase 3, East Africa: Kenya, Rwanda and Tanzania, reusing the same platform and adding each country's exams, admission rules and pay scales.

## Risks: 3 biggest risks + mitigation (200 word limit)

> 1. Wrong information could mislead students. A wrong cut-off or pay figure can cost a student a year. Mitigation: we use official sources only, show the source and date beside every figure, label anything unverified, and publish a calculator only when the admissions body publishes its formula. Where data is missing we say "not yet published" instead of guessing.
>
> 2. Schools may adopt slowly or not pay. Schools have small budgets and many priorities. Mitigation: the product is free for students, so schools can try it with no risk; we start with a pilot that proves value to teachers, price per student so small schools pay little, and add revenue from universities and employers so schools are not our only payers.
>
> 3. Children's data and regulation. Many users are minors. Mitigation: today we collect no personal data: no accounts, no cookies, no tracking, and the CV builder keeps data on the user's device. Before accounts launch we will register with Uganda's Personal Data Protection Office and Kenya's Data Commissioner, keep profiles minimal and record consent. Draft privacy and terms pages are already published.

## Social Impact: how you're making a difference 🌍 (200 word limit)

> Across Africa, millions of young people choose subjects, courses and careers with almost no reliable information. A subject choice at 16 can quietly close the door to medicine or engineering. Admission rules are hidden in PDFs. Career websites quote American salaries. Job seekers are targeted by recruiters who charge illegal fees.
>
> CareerProof Global gives every student the same quality of guidance that well-resourced schools provide, free, on a basic smartphone, with no sign-up. A student in a rural school can see every route to the career they want, the subjects and qualifications that open it, which universities offer it, and what it actually pays in their own country, from official figures.
>
> We focus on fairness and safety: our tools never predict a student's "chance" of admission, they show the official rules and past cut-offs so students can judge for themselves. Our job-market guide explains the official rules on recruitment fees and how to check a recruiter, using sources from Uganda's labour ministry, South Africa, the UK, the US and the ILO.
>
> Better choices mean fewer wasted school years and fees, more students in courses that lead to work, and a workforce that matches what Africa's economies need.

## Competitive Landscape: who else is out there + how you're different (200 word limit)

> Students today piece guidance together from four places:
>
> 1. Official portals (university portals, KUCCPS in Kenya) are built for applying, one system at a time. They do not help a student explore careers or see which subjects lead where.
> 2. Cut-off lists on social media and in newspapers are scattered, often unverified, and rarely explain how points are calculated.
> 3. Global career and salary sites (O*NET, Prospects, Indeed, PayScale) are built for the US and Europe. They quote foreign pay, often from user-reported estimates, and ignore African exams, regulators and institutions.
> 4. Careers teachers are few and overstretched. **[FILL IN: local competitors you know of, e.g. Ugandan or Kenyan career apps, and how they differ]**
>
> How we are different:
>
> - Routes, not lists: we map every path from where a student is to where they want to be, including career changes, not one career page at a time.
> - Official data only, cited: pay from public salary scales and national statistics, admission rules from the bodies that set them. No guessed odds.
> - Africa-first, global by design: African exams, regulators and pay scales on the ILO and UNESCO standards national statistics offices use.
>
> Our advantage grows with each country: an assembled, licence-checked dataset of 72 sources that is slow for others to rebuild.
